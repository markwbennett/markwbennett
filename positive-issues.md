## A. Textual differences the lockstep era erased

Article I text differs from the federal text in places, and the dark-ages courts
declared the provisions equivalent without reading the words. These four are the
ones where the difference does work.

### 1. "Give evidence against himself" — art. I, §10

**Rule as applied.** Coextensive with the Fifth Amendment's "be a witness against
himself": testimonial evidence only, so blood draws, breath tests, handwriting
exemplars and lineups are unprotected `[verify: the CCA holding, likely early
1970s, that §10 tracks *Schmerber*]`.

**The gap.** The Texas text says **give evidence**, not *be a witness*. "Evidence"
in 1876 was not a synonym for testimony; Greenleaf's treatise is titled *On
Evidence* and covers real and documentary proof. A citizen compelled to bleed
into a vial gives evidence against himself in the ordinary 1876 sense.

**Why it matters.** Every DWI blood draw, breath test and refusal in the state.
This is the highest-volume textual argument available, and it has the same shape
as due course versus due process — different words, chosen, never read.

### 2. "Possessions" — art. I, §9

**Rule as applied.** Texas follows *Oliver*'s open-fields doctrine and the federal
standing rules `[verify]`.

**The gap.** The Fourth Amendment protects "effects"; §9 protects **possessions**.
Land is a possession; "effects" has never been read to include it. The open-fields
doctrine rests on the federal word. A defendant who *possesses* property he does
not own — a borrowed car, a rented room, a friend's phone — also has a textual
claim to standing the federal "reasonable expectation" test denies.

### 3. "Cruel *or* unusual" — art. I, §13

**Rule as applied.** Coextensive with the Eighth Amendment `[verify: *Cantu v.
State*, late 1990s]`.

**The gap.** The Eighth Amendment is conjunctive; §13 is disjunctive. A punishment
that is merely **unusual** — contrary to long usage, which is what the word meant
in 1876 — violates §13 without also being cruel. Candidates: stacked sentences of
a length no 1876 court could impose, sex-offender conditions with no historical
analogue, punishments for offenses that did not exist. The originalist meaning of
"unusual" is a real literature on the federal side and has never been applied to
the Texas disjunctive.

### 4. "Confronted *with* the witnesses" — art. I, §10

**Rule as applied.** Coextensive with the Confrontation Clause; *Maryland v. Craig*
governs child-witness closed-circuit testimony, and post-2020 practice has
normalized remote testimony `[verify Texas cases]`.

**The gap.** Same words since 1836 — which is exactly the situation the Texas
readoption canon addresses (*Robertson*). What did "confronted with" require in
Texas practice between 1836 and 1876? If it meant physical presence in the
courtroom, then two-way video is a departure the readopted text does not permit,
and *Craig* is a federal floor, not a Texas ceiling.

## B. Lockstep imports never re-examined after *Heitman*

### 5. *Atwater* under art. I, §9

**Rule as applied.** Custodial arrest for a fine-only offense is constitutional
(*Atwater v. Lago Vista*, 2001).

**The gap.** The leading Supreme Court case arose in Texas — and because Atwater
sued in federal court, **the Texas constitutional question was never decided**.
Was custodial arrest for a fine-only, non-breach-of-peace offense permitted at
common law in 1876? Blackstone and the arrest treatises say warrantless arrest lay
for felony and for breach of the peace in the officer's presence; a seatbelt
violation is neither. Art. 14.06's citation-release provision is the statutory
companion. `[verify whether the CCA has addressed §9 post-*Atwater*, and whether
the 1876 argument was made]`

### 6. Community caretaking

**Rule as applied.** Texas adopted *Cady v. Dombrowski*'s community-caretaking
exception (*Wright v. State*, 1999 `[verify]`), permitting a seizure with no
suspicion of any offense.

**The gap.** The common law recognized no authority to seize a person absent an
offense; the doctrine is a 1973 federal innovation adopted by a Texas court that
performed no Texas analysis. It is the cleanest possible target: no offense, no
warrant, no Texas pedigree.

### 7. Pretext

**Rule as applied.** *Whren*; Texas declined a pretext inquiry under §9
(*Crittenden v. State*, 1995 `[verify]`).

**The gap.** The 1876 common law on using an arrest for one purpose to search for
another. If the common law condemned the practice, *Crittenden* is a dark-ages
holding in post-*Heitman* clothing — decided four years after *Heitman* without
doing what *Heitman* authorized.

## C. Manner-or-means, *Apprendi*, *Ramos*

### 8. Continuous sexual abuse — Penal Code §21.02(d)

**Rule as applied.** The statute itself says jurors "are not required to agree
unanimously on which specific acts of sexual abuse were committed." Courts of
appeals have rejected *Richardson v. United States* challenges on the ground that
the acts are "morally and conceptually equivalent" — the *Schad* plurality's
reasoning `[verify; a cert petition, No. 20-973, was filed in January 2021]`.

**The gap.** *Richardson* held that for a federal "series of violations" the jury
must agree on which violations. The Texas courts' answer is *Schad*'s
moral-equivalence test — which is precisely the reasoning *Apprendi* and *Ramos*
displaced, per Section 7 of the manifesto. And if the rejections are courts of
appeals citing each other, the question has been laundered, not settled. Run the
four-part test: has the **CCA** rejected the *Apprendi/Ramos* version after full
briefing? The stakes are 25-to-life with no parole.

### 9. The judge-made family-violence finding

**Rule as applied.** A trial judge makes an affirmative finding of family violence
under art. 42.013; that finding later elevates a misdemeanor assault to a felony
under §22.01(b)(2)(A) `[verify the CCA's *Apprendi* treatment, possibly *Butler v.
State*]`.

**The gap.** *Apprendi*'s prior-conviction exception covers the fact of a prior
conviction. The family-violence *finding* is a separate fact, found by a judge,
not an element of the prior offense, and it increases the punishment range in the
later case. That is the *Apprendi* pattern with the exception's protection
removed. Enormous volume.

### 10. DWI's two definitions of intoxication

**Rule as applied.** "Loss of normal use" (§49.01(2)(A)) and 0.08 (§49.01(2)(B))
are alternative manner-or-means; no unanimity required `[verify: *Bagheri*]`.

**The gap.** Impairment and per-se concentration are different facts proved by
different evidence — one by observation, one by a machine. The manner-or-means
label was applied without the element analysis the manifesto describes. Same
argument as §19.02(b), in the highest-volume offense in the state.

### 11. Aggravated assault's two aggravators — and the "gravamen" root

**Rule as applied.** Serious bodily injury (§22.02(a)(1)) and use of a deadly
weapon (§22.02(a)(2)) are treated under the CCA's "gravamen" jurisprudence
(*Jefferson*, *Landrian*, *Pizzo* `[verify]`).

**The gap.** The gravamen test is judge-made and is the **root proposition** for a
whole family of manner-or-means holdings. It is the natural target for the
root-case census: find where the gravamen test was first announced, read what
that case actually decided, and ask whether the test survives *Apprendi*'s
definition of an element.

## D. Mens rea reach — *Flores-Figueroa* and *Rehaif*

### 12. Felon in possession — Penal Code §46.04

**Rule as applied.** No mental state as to felon status; §6.02 supplies a mental
state for the conduct `[verify CCA treatment]`.

**The gap.** *Rehaif v. United States* (2019) held the federal statute requires
knowledge of the prohibited status. Texas §46.04 states no mental state at all, so
§6.02(b)–(c) supplies one — and the question is whether the supplied mental state
reaches "has been convicted of a felony." The recurring fact pattern is real:
defendants on deferred adjudication, or with out-of-state dispositions, who do
not know their status. Pair with the penalty-group grammar argument; it is the
same move applied to a status element.

### 13. THC concentrate versus marihuana

**Rule as applied.** The Johnson argument, applied to the vape-pen cases.

**The gap.** Marihuana is a Class B misdemeanor; THC concentrate is Penalty Group
2 and a felony. The defendant who believed the cartridge was marijuana did not
"knowingly possess a controlled substance listed in Penalty Group 2." This is the
manifesto's own argument at its highest volume and with its most sympathetic
facts — a teenager with a vape pen — and it should be in the pipeline alongside
*Johnson* rather than after it.

### 14. Failure to register — art. 62.102

**Rule as applied.** "Knowingly" fails to comply; knowledge of the *duty* is not
required `[verify]`.

**The gap.** Same grammar: "knowingly" modifies the failure to comply with a
requirement, and one cannot knowingly fail to comply with a requirement one does
not know exists. The federal SORNA analogue has generated the same argument.

## E. Dormant statutory text

### 15. Art. 1.051's right to counsel

**Rule as applied.** The Texas statutory right to counsel attaches when the Sixth
Amendment right attaches `[verify]`.

**The gap.** Art. 1.051 grants counsel "in an adversarial judicial proceeding" and
art. 15.17 makes magistration a proceeding with the accused before a judicial
officer. If the statute's words reach magistration, the Texas right attaches
earlier than *Rothgery*'s federal floor, and uncounseled statements and waivers
between arrest and appointment are a statutory violation with a 38.23 remedy. A
dormant-text argument of the art. 15.22 kind.
