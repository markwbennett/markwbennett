# Narrowed: appeals, writs, and PDRs

A cutting-edge artisanal appellate practice — direct appeals, petitions for
discretionary review, and writs.

## What the three-part scope does to the vocabulary

Adding appeals and PDRs to writs moves the center of gravity. A pure
post-conviction name can live on *habeas / coram nobis / vacatur*. A practice
that also files briefs in the courts of appeals and petitions in the Court of
Criminal Appeals needs a word that spans all three, and there are only two
families that do:

- **the instrument** — brief, petition, writ: the thing you draft
- **the record and its errors** — the raw material, identical in all three postures

*Review* is the third candidate and the most PDR-specific, since a PDR literally
presents "grounds for review."

## What "artisanal" does to the suffix

Maker suffixes — **wright, smith, forge, foundry, bench, works, press** — over
`labs` and `stack`. For an appellate practice the lab problem from the earlier
list is milder than it was for pure post-conviction (nobody thinks a brief comes
out of a laboratory), but the maker words are still the better fit: appellate
work *is* small-batch, and the craft framing is honest rather than decorative.

## The narrowed list

### 1. writwright.com

A *wright* is one who makes — wheelwright, shipwright, playwright — and you make
the instruments. Real word-formation, not a coinage, and it covers writs
explicitly while a brief and a petition are the same craft.

Screen: clear. **But know the neighborhood:** `briefwright.com` is already taken
by Rankin Johnson IV, a criminal-appellate lawyer in Portland, Oregon. The
`-wright` construction has occurred to someone in exactly this niche, so it is
not virgin territory — it is, however, a different string in a different state,
and "writ" is the more precise root for a practice that includes 11.07 work.

### 2. groundsforreview.com

In a Texas PDR you present *grounds for review*. The phrase is a term of art that
names the discretionary-review half of the practice exactly, and it carries a
plain-English second meaning — you have grounds — that a client understands
without a glossary. It also reads fine over an appeal or a writ.

Screen: clear; searches turn up only lawyer-review-site marketing noise.

### 3. recordandbrief.com

The two things an appellate court receives, and the two things you live inside.
It reads like a bindery or a law stationer's shopfront, which is the artisanal
register done straight rather than ironically. Spans all three postures: every
one of them is a record and a brief.

Screen: clear; only library research guides use the phrase.

### 4. coldrecord.com (or thecoldrecord.com)

Still strong, and it survives the scope change better than anything else from the
post-conviction list: appeal, PDR, and writ are all worked from the cold record.
Screen: "The Cold Record" is a Rude Mechs theater piece, plus unrelated music
labels — no domain occupant found.

### 5. pointoferror.com

A Texas term of art (points of error, now styled issues presented) — which makes
it quietly nostalgic to any Texas appellate lawyer and opaque to everyone else.
The risk is that it names the trial court's mistake rather than your work.
Screen: clear, dictionary entries only.

### 6. preservederror.com

What separates a winnable appeal from a sympathetic one. Insider, precise, a
little austere. Screen: clear.

### 7. enbanc.co

Short, Latin, and it sounds like a modern company in the way `vacatur` does.
Narrower than the others — en banc is a rehearing posture, not the practice — but
the shortest good string here. Screen: no company found; `enbanc.com` is short
enough to be registered regardless.

### 8. vacatur.co / vacaturworks.com

Carried over. Still names the remedy, still screened clear, still the best option
if you want the name to state the outcome you seek rather than the craft. Slightly
off-center now that direct appeals and PDRs are in scope.

### Also clear, if flatter

`appellatewright.com`, `brieffoundry.com`, `writfoundry.com`, `writsmith.com`,
`coramworks.com`, `reversibleerror.com`.

## Cut

- **briefwright.com** — taken, by a criminal-appellate lawyer (Portland, OR).
- **briefsmith** — `briefsmith.ai` is an SEO content-brief tool.
- **standardofreview.com** — "The Standard of Review" is an existing appellate
  podcast (Corrie Woods, Pennsylvania Supreme Court practice). The phrase is
  spoken for in this exact professional niche.
- **errata\*** — Errata Security (erratasec.com), an Atlanta infosec firm founded
  2006, owns the errata-in-tech association.
- **writ.works** — occupied by a writer/facilitator.
- **habeas\*** — habeas.ai is a legal-research AI vendor.
- **elevenoseven.com** — at least two companies trade as "Eleven O Seven."
- **doubt / voir dire names** — trial vocabulary; wrong end of the case.

## One to avoid on purpose

**reviewgranted.com** and anything like it. It is the best-sounding name in the
whole exercise and you should not use it: a firm name announcing the relief you
seek is squarely what the not-misleading half of Texas Rule 7.01 is aimed at —
a statement substantially likely to create unjustified expectations about
results. Same objection to *acquittal*-as-promise names for a trial practice.

## Pick

**writwright.com**, with **groundsforreview.com** as the close second — and
groundsforreview is the better choice if PDR work is the part of the practice you
most want to be known for, since it is the only candidate that names discretionary
review specifically.
