# Twelve research paths

Extensions of the three engines in *Making Law*: opinion laundering (find the root),
due course of law (the 1876 common-law incorporation), and manner-or-means versus
elements. Each path gives the hypothesis, the search that tests it, and what would
kill it.

**Citations not drawn from the manifesto are flagged `[verify]` — I have not read
them.** Nothing here is a substitute for running the searches.

---

## A. Scaling the dark-ages audit

### 1. Inventory every lockstep import, not just Terry

**Hypothesis.** *Terry*/*Milton* is one instance of a class. Between *Crowell*
(1944) and *Heitman* (1991) the CCA imported federal doctrine wholesale across
search and seizure, self-incrimination, confrontation, double jeopardy, jury trial
and ex post facto. Each import rests on no Texas constitutional analysis, and
*Heitman* reopened all of them — but only the search-and-seizure door has been
tried.

**Search.** Pre-1991 CCA opinions containing "in all material aspects the same" and
its variants; then every case citing *Crowell* or its progeny for the proposition
that a Texas provision means what its federal analog means. Build the list; sort by
how much modern practice rests on each.

**Kills it.** An import that the CCA has re-examined post-*Heitman* on independent
Texas grounds is spent. Check each for a post-1991 independent analysis before
investing.

### 2. The textual-difference inventory

**Hypothesis.** Lockstep interpretation erased textual differences between article I
and the Bill of Rights. Where Texas chose different words, those words were chosen —
"due course" versus "due process" is the proven case, and it will not be the only one.

**Search.** Set article I beside the federal provisions clause by clause. For every
divergence in wording, find the case that declared them equivalent and read whether
it addressed the text at all. The 1875 convention debates and the 1876 ratification
materials are the originalist support.

**Kills it.** A divergence that predates statehood and was copied from another state's
constitution without deliberation is weaker — though still worth the common-law pedigree
argument.

### 3. Borrow from the other "law of the land" states

**Hypothesis.** Texas is not the only state whose nineteenth-century constitution
incorporated common-law criminal procedure. Other state courts have done the
originalist work and assembled the treatise citations already.

**Search.** State constitutions using "law of the land" or "due course of law";
then those states' cases holding the clause incorporates common-law procedure. Their
briefs and opinions are a shortcut to sources, and out-of-state authority costs
nothing to cite as persuasive.

**Kills it.** States whose courts read the clause as identical to federal due process
give you nothing but a contrary citation — know which those are before relying on the family.

## B. Scaling the 1791–1876 window

### 4. Enumerate the window's protections from the treatises

**Hypothesis.** Footnote 6 generalizes. The reasonable-hypothesis instruction is one
protection that crystallized after 1791 and before 1876; the treatises describe dozens.
Each one modern Texas law no longer provides is a due-course-of-law argument.

**Search.** Work Greenleaf, Starkie, Bishop and Wharton systematically rather than for
one rule — table of contents first, then every procedural protection described as
settled. For each, ask: does Texas still provide it? The ones that vanished are the
inventory.

**Kills it.** A protection abolished by *statute* rather than by courts raises the
harder question of legislative power below the constitutional floor. Sort those into a
separate, slower pile.

### 5. The vanished jury instructions

**Hypothesis.** *Hankins* is one of a family. Nineteenth-century Texas juries were
given cautionary instructions that no longer appear in any charge, each abolished by
judicial decision rather than legislative amendment.

**Search.** Pre-1900 Texas opinions via `DA(bef 1900)` reciting jury charges; catalogue
the instructions given as a matter of course. Then trace each forward to the decision
that dropped it. Cross-check which survive by statute (accomplice-witness corroboration
is statutory, so it lives) versus which were purely common-law.

**Kills it.** An instruction that was always discretionary, or that the 1876 courts
themselves split on, will not carry the "settled common law" premise.

### 6. Indictment and pleading practice in 1876

**Hypothesis.** The manner-or-means pleading the State relies on — six methods in one
paragraph — is downstream of a modern relaxation of common-law indictment
requirements. At common law an indictment had to allege the offense with particularity.

**Search.** The treatises on indictment sufficiency; early Texas cases quashing
indictments for failure to specify. Then the twentieth-century Texas cases that relaxed
it, and whether any addressed article I, section 10's notice requirement independently.

**Kills it.** Statutory codification of pleading rules; if the Legislature authorized
the practice, the argument becomes a constitutional-floor argument rather than a
statutory one.

## C. Scaling manner-or-means and mens rea

### 7. The Penal Code verb-list sweep

**Hypothesis.** §19.02(b) and §43.04(a) are examples of a finite, enumerable class:
every offense defined with a list of alternative verbs or circumstances is a candidate
for distinct-element treatment and unanimity.

**Search.** Mechanical, and the highest-yield project here. Walk the Penal Code and the
Health and Safety Code offense definitions; build a table of every offense with
alternatives in the definition; mark which have been litigated as unanimity questions
and which have not. Most will not have been. Rank by frequency of prosecution.

**Kills it.** Where the Legislature grouped alternatives that are genuinely morally
equivalent, *Schad* is a real obstacle — pick the ones where the verbs describe
different conduct by different actors.

### 8. The "knowingly" scope sweep

**Hypothesis.** The penalty-group argument is *Flores-Figueroa* applied to one statute.
Any offense where a mental state precedes a list including a circumstance element has
the same structure, and the pattern charge probably drops the circumstance.

**Search.** Offense definitions where a culpable mental state is followed by an object
containing a qualifying phrase (a classification, a status, an age, a value, a location).
For each, read the pattern charge's application paragraph and see whether the qualifier
survives.

**Kills it.** Where the Legislature separately defined the circumstance as a strict-
liability aggravator, the grammar argument weakens — but that itself is an Apprendi
question.

### 9. Audit the pattern charges against the statutes

**Hypothesis.** The State Bar pattern charges are a finite corpus, and every divergence
between an application paragraph and its statute is a potential egregious-harm argument.
Two divergences are already documented (penalty group, trespass consent). There is no
reason to think there are only two.

**Search.** Line up each pattern charge beside its offense definition and diff them.
This is grunt work with a very high hit rate, and it produces trial-level arguments that
require no appellate posture to deploy.

**Kills it.** Divergences that *narrow* the offense favor the defense and should be left
alone.

## D. Scaling the laundering detector

### 10. The root-case census

**Hypothesis.** Laundered lines have a signature: a root case with enormous in-degree
whose own discussion of the proposition is a sentence or two, and descendants that cite
the root rather than reasoning independently.

**Search.** Pick a doctrine, pull the citator's full citing list, and sort by depth of
treatment. The nodes with thousands of citations and shallow treatment in the root are
the candidates. Then read the root. *Aguirre* is the proof of concept; the method is
general and mostly mechanical.

**Kills it.** A root whose reasoning is actually adequate — which happens, and finding
out early is cheap.

### 11. The dormant-statute sweep

**Hypothesis.** Article 15.22 is not the only statute a Texas court has declared
obsolete without a repeal. Each such statute is dormant: still binding, waiting to be
applied.

**Search.** Opinions containing "legislatively obsolete," "no longer viable," "has been
superseded," "of no further effect," and similar, paired with a statutory citation.
Then read the statute and ask what it would do if applied as written.

**Kills it.** An actual repeal or amendment. Check the statutory history first — it takes
a minute and saves the argument from embarrassment.

### 12. Abolitions that parallel *Hankins*

**Hypothesis.** The *Hankins* argument — that the court lacked power to abolish a
constitutionally incorporated protection — applies to every Texas-specific protection the
CCA abolished by decision rather than by legislative amendment.

**Search.** Cases in which the CCA eliminated a defense-favorable Texas rule and joined a
federal or majority trend. The abolition of factual-sufficiency review in *Brooks v.
State* `[verify: 323 S.W.3d 893 (Tex. Crim. App. 2010) (plurality)]` is the obvious
candidate and structurally identical: a Texas-specific protection, abolished by the
court, justified by reference to the federal standard rather than to Texas constitutional
analysis. Ask the same question — was the protection part of the common law of 1876, or
otherwise beyond the court's power to remove?

**Kills it.** A protection that originated in a statute later repealed, or one the court
can fairly characterize as its own procedural innovation, never had constitutional
footing to begin with.

## Sequencing

Paths 7, 8 and 9 are mechanical and produce case inventories rather than arguments —
they are the cheapest way to generate a pipeline, and a law student can do most of the
work. Paths 1, 4 and 5 are the research programs that would take a year and produce a
decade of arguments. Path 12 is the one with an existing, obvious target and the
argument already written in Section 5 of the manifesto.
