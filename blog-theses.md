# Ten theses — blog posts for a lawyer audience, educational voice

Each is a **thesis** (an arguable proposition) rather than a topic. Sequenced as
a series: 1–3 name the problem, 4–6 give the tools, 7–8 cover the vectors, 9–10
handle the procedural reality of doing anything about it.

Every case reference below is an **unverified lead** from web search, not read.

---

## 1. Opinion laundering: how a proposition becomes law without ever being decided

**Thesis:** A rule can enter Texas law with no authority behind it and become
unassailable purely through repetition. Court B cites A for something A never
held; C cites B; at fifty years the proposition is "well settled" and its origin
is unrecoverable to anyone who has not walked the chain back.

The flagship post — it defines your term and everything else in the series hangs
off it. Needs one worked example carried all the way to the source, which is the
whole persuasive burden. Distinguish it up front from *citation laundering*, now
in circulation for AI-hallucinated cases passing through real filings; yours is
the older and worse problem, because the cases are real.

## 2. "Well settled" is a citation count, not an argument

**Thesis:** In an appellate brief the phrase almost always marks the place where
reasoning stopped. A proposition that has been reasoned to gets reasoned to
again; a proposition that has been laundered gets a string cite.

Teaches a reflex: when the State writes "it is well settled," treat it as a flag
rather than a wall, and go look for the last court that actually reasoned to the
rule. Pairs naturally with the Virginia Law Review treatment of what "settled"
even means — undisturbed for fifty years, entrenched by stare decisis, or widely
accepted, none of which is *correct*.

## 3. Read what they cited

**Thesis:** The highest-yield habit in appellate practice is the least
sophisticated one — pulling the case at the end of the string cite and reading
what it actually held.

The practical post of the series. Show the mechanics: take the foundational case
in a line, pull everything citing it, and look for the tell — the original
disposed of the point in a sentence with no reasoning, and every case since cites
the original instead of reasoning independently. This is *tracing*, and the
money-laundering parallel is worth making explicit: the taint survives the
transfers.

## 4. "Assuming without deciding" is a searchable index of open questions

**Thesis:** The phrases courts use to avoid deciding — *assuming without
deciding*, *we need not decide*, *assuming arguendo* — constitute a public map of
questions nobody has been made to answer, and you can query it.

Give the actual searches. Every one of those phrases in a line of cases is a
question the court stepped over; where the step-over repeats across decades, the
underlying question has never been properly briefed to anyone. This is the post
that hands readers a tool they can use the same afternoon.

## 5. Per incuriam: the name for a thing we do without naming

**Thesis:** English courts have a doctrine for a decision rendered in ignorance
of a binding authority or statute — *per incuriam*, from *Young v Bristol
Aeroplane Co Ltd* [1944] KB 718 — and Texas has the same idea sitting unlabeled
inside its stare decisis framework.

Educational in the purest sense: most Texas criminal lawyers have never
encountered the term. Worth confirming first whether any Texas court has used it
(full-text `"per incuriam"` in TX-CS or Google Scholar); an empty result makes
the post *more* interesting, not less. Close on the etymology: *incuria* is
*in-* + *cura*, want of care — and *curious* descends from the same *cura*. The
opposite of a decision made per incuriam is one made by a curious lawyer.

## 6. The overruling factors are a brief template, not an obstacle

**Thesis:** Most briefs asking a court to abandon precedent never address the
court's own stated criteria for doing so, and therefore lose before they start.

Search summaries attribute a three-factor test to *Hammock v. State*, 46 S.W.3d
889, 892-93 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001): the rule was flawed from the outset; it
conflicts with newer and more soundly reasoned precedent; it consistently
produces unjust results or burdens the system. **Verify the pin cite and the
factors before writing** — I have not read the page. The post writes itself as a
structure: one heading per factor.

## 7. Dicta do not become holdings, but citation makes them behave that way

**Thesis:** The most common laundering vector is a court's aside, repeated until
practitioners treat it as a rule — and the cure is showing the court that the
sentence it is being asked to follow was never necessary to any judgment.

Distinct from post 1: that one is about propositions with no source; this one is
about propositions with a real source that was never load-bearing. Different
attack, different proof.

## 8. Unpublished dispositions launder propositions they cannot create

**Thesis:** Opinions that bind nobody still shape practice, because practitioners
read them as signals and then brief accordingly, and the resulting consensus
gets cited back to courts as though it were law.

There is a St. Mary's Law Journal treatment of Tex. R. App. P. 90(i) and the
contours of non-citable authority worth mining for the history. The teaching
point is practical: know which propositions in your area exist only in memoranda.

## 9. Preserving the argument nobody has made

**Thesis:** Fifty-year-old bad rules survive partly because the lawyers who
finally spot the flaw lose it on preservation — and preserving a novel argument
is a different craft from preserving a familiar one.

The most immediately useful post for trial lawyers, and the one that makes the
series matter to people who will never file a PDR. Mechanics: what has to be said
in the trial court to keep an unmade argument alive, how specific the objection
must be when the authority you need does not exist yet, and how to carry the
issue up without narrowing it.

## 10. A PDR is an institutional argument, not a second appeal

**Thesis:** PDRs fail because they argue that the court of appeals was wrong,
when the question is whether the issue is worth the Court of Criminal Appeals'
attention — and those are different documents.

Teaches grounds for review as a claim about the law rather than about the case,
including the percolation point: the value of an issue that has produced
inconsistent results across courts of appeals, and how to show it. The natural
closer, because it is where everything in posts 1–9 gets cashed.
