# What the manifesto and the plan are not thinking about

Ordered by how much damage each could do. Items marked `[verify]` rest on search
results, not on opinions I have read.

## Threats to the argument

### 1. The freeze date is wrong, or at least contestable — and the State will find it

Section 5 says the framers "chose different words in 1876" and thereby adopted the
common law of criminal procedure "as it existed in 1876." But "due course of the
law of the land" was not chosen in 1876. It appears in the **1836 Republic
Constitution** (Declaration of Rights, §7) and the **1845 Constitution** (art. I,
§16) in nearly identical words, and it descends from Magna Carta clause 39 by way
of a dozen other state constitutions. `[verify against the Tarlton texts]`

Consequences:
- The "deliberate choice" premise needs restating. The 1876 framers *readopted*
  inherited language; the argument has to be that readoption carries the 1876
  understanding, or that the relevant date is whenever Texas first adopted the
  phrase — and those lead different places.
- **Footnote 6 is exposed.** *Hodge's Case* is 1838. If the operative adoption is
  1836, the reasonable-hypothesis rule crystallized *after* Texas adopted "due
  course," and the "before 1876, after 1791" window closes for that rule. If the
  date is 1845 or 1876, it holds. The paper should pick a date and defend it before
  the State picks the other one.
- Consider a second anchor: the Republic's statutory adoption of the common law of
  England as the rule of decision (the 1840 act) `[verify]`. It may be the better
  vehicle for "Texas adopted the common law" than the constitutional phrase alone.

This is the root-case problem applied to the manifesto's own root. Do the
archaeology on yourself before the State does.

**Refinement, after pushback:** readoption in 1876 is the right frame, and it
rescues *Hodge's Case* — "the window closes" overstated it; the window closes
only if the court fixes the date at 1836, and readoption supplies a principled
basis for 1876. But Texas's readoption canon is specifically about **judicial
construction**: where a provision that has received a settled judicial
construction is adopted in the same words by a later constitution, the
construction is presumed adopted too (applied to art. I, §10 confrontation, same
words since 1836 — *Robertson v. State* `[verify]`). That is a tool for both
sides. The decisive research question is therefore narrow: **what construction had
"due course of the law of the land" received in Texas cases between 1836 and
1876?** If those courts applied common-law procedure under it, the canon carries
that forward. If they read it as a generic law-of-the-land guarantee, the State
gets the canon.

**And a statutory hook that may be better than the constitutional one:** Tex.
Code Crim. Proc. art. 1.27, "Common Law Governs" — "If this Code fails to provide
a rule of procedure in any particular state of case which may arise, the rules of
the common law shall be applied and govern." Current law. It requires no proof of
a constitutional freeze; the Legislature itself directs that common-law procedure
governs the gaps. Limits: gap-filling only (it yields to codified procedure), and
amendable. But for questions the Code does not address it is a direct textual
argument. The *Terry* argument, for instance, becomes purely statutory: art. 15.22
defines arrest as binary, the Code is silent on any sub-arrest detention, art. 1.27
sends that silence to the common law, and the common law had no such category.
The one open question is *which* common law art. 1.27 incorporates — as of the
Code's adoption, or as it evolves — and that question is worth a post of its own.

### 2. *Hankins* and *Geesa* are two abolitions, and Section 5 runs them together

*Hankins* (1983) abolished the **jury instruction**. *Geesa v. State*, 820 S.W.2d
154 (Tex. Crim. App. 1991) abolished the **reasonable-hypothesis analytical
construct for sufficiency review** `[verify]`. The paper's stated practical
consequence — "every circumstantial-evidence conviction is reviewable under it" —
is a *Geesa* consequence, not a *Hankins* one. And the Bishop passages quoted are
about sufficiency ("the test of the sufficiency of this evidence"), which supports
reviving the *Geesa* construct more directly than the *Hankins* charge.

Two roots, two arguments, two vehicles. Either revive both separately or say which
one, because the State's first move will be to point out that the authorities cited
support the one the paper is not asking for.

### 3. The incorporation premise is asserted, not proven

The treatises prove what the common law *was*. Nothing yet proves that "due course
of law" froze it as a constitutional floor rather than adopting it as a default
rule courts could revise. That is the load-bearing proposition of Sections 5, 6 and
the 1791–1876 window. It needs: the 1875 convention journal, contemporaneous Texas
cases construing "due course," and the other "law of the land" states' treatment of
the same question. Without it, the State's answer is one sentence: the phrase means
what "due process" means, as Texas courts said for a century.

### 4. Floodgates is the court's real objection, and the paper does not answer it

"Every circumstantial-evidence conviction is reviewable" is written as a selling
point. To the Court of Criminal Appeals it is the reason to refuse. A holding that
*Hankins* was void from 1983 unravels four decades of convictions; judges weigh
that whether or not they say so. The argument needs a **remedial theory**: Texas
retroactivity doctrine for new rules on habeas, a prospective-only fallback the
court can grant without opening 1983, and a reason the court should prefer a
narrow first step. Offer the off-ramp or the court will build its own, and it will
be a refusal.

## Threats to the strategy

### 5. Statutory wins are wasting assets

The penalty-group "knowingly" argument, the trespass consent argument, the
§19.02(b) and §43.04(a) unanimity arguments are wins on **statutory text**. The
Legislature meets in January 2027 and can amend any of them into strict liability
or a single offense in one session. Wins on the Texas Constitution cannot be
amended away. The paper already prefers constitutional grounds; the *positioning*
should say so explicitly, and the litigation calendar should assume the State will
ask the Legislature to fix anything that loses.

### 6. There is no forcing mechanism against serial refusal — so manufacture the split

The CCA refused *Solis*, *Earl* and *Riley*. Refusals cost the court nothing and
it can keep refusing. Rule 66.3(a) makes a **conflict among courts of appeals** a
ground for review. *Solis* is in the Fourteenth, *Williams* in the Fourth,
*Johnson* in Dallas — if that distribution is not deliberate, make it deliberate:
put the same issue before different courts, with different panels, until one
splits. A split is the one thing the court cannot refuse indefinitely.

### 7. The federal backstop is asymmetric

The First Amendment work has a real path to the Supreme Court (*Earl* proves it).
The unanimity work does not: *Ramos* requires unanimity on *elements*, but what
counts as an element is state law under *Schad*, and AEDPA deference will defeat a
§2254 claim that a Texas offense definition creates two elements. The unanimity
arguments are **Texas** arguments and should be built to win in Texas, with the
federal overlay as reinforcement rather than as the backstop.

### 8. The Supreme Court of Texas is a flank

The CCA is not the only Texas court reading article I, §19. *Patel v. Texas
Department of Licensing*, 469 S.W.3d 69 (Tex. 2015) held that the due-course clause
has independent content and applied a standard stricter than federal rational
basis `[verify]` — though it assumed rather than decided that the clause applied,
and did not reach the originalist question. The premise "due course of law is not
lockstep with due process" can be strengthened in *civil* cases, where the State
cares less and the Supreme Court of Texas has been aggressively originalist. A
civil holding that the 1876 clause carries the 1876 understanding is persuasive
authority the CCA cannot ignore.

### 9. Publishing moves the *Strickland* baseline

Once the manifesto is public, "no reasonable lawyer would have thought to raise
it" stops being available as a reason failure-to-preserve was not deficient. That
cuts both ways:
- It creates **11.07 vehicles** — ineffective-assistance claims for post-publication
  trials where counsel did not preserve the Texas Constitution objection.
- It creates **exposure for the bar you are recruiting**, and for you, since
  courts generally hold counsel is not deficient for failing to anticipate a change
  in the law — but the paper's whole thesis is that this is not a change in the
  law, it is law that was always there.

Think about whether to say this out loud, because it is the strongest possible
reason for a trial lawyer to make the objection.

### 10. Amici

Novel arguments land with institutional backing. The *Earl* cert petition needs
amici (TCDLA, the First Amendment shops, law professors); so will the first
due-course-of-law PDR the court grants. Lining them up is a year's lead time.

## Threats to the business

### 11. Twelve-year projects need a funding structure

*Owens* took twelve years. Impact litigation is nonprofit-funded because clients
cannot pay for a decade of PDRs. If Bennett & Bennett funds this from fee-paying
appellate work, it is a subsidy with no end date. The naming thread objected to
"Institute" because the word implies a nonprofit — but that is the answer, not the
problem: an Institute that *is* a nonprofit can take grants, run fellowships, and
partner with a law-school clinic whose students do the mechanical sweeps (research
paths 7–10) for credit. The status question I raised becomes the business model.

### 12. The referral base is the audience being called cookie-cutters

The appellate practice depends on trial lawyers preserving issues and sending
cases. The manifesto's tone toward them is a choice — it is the Defending People
voice and it works — but the ask ("name the constitution, name the provision") is
a recruitment, and recruitment lands better than indictment. The blog series to
lawyers should make trial counsel the heroes of the preservation story, because
they are.

### 13. Nobody asked who the client is

The whole naming exercise assumed a lawyer audience. If families of convicted
people googling "appeal lawyer Houston" are a meaningful channel, everything
erudite on the site is invisible to them and bennettandbennett.com needs a
plain-English layer that says, in one screen, what an appeal is, what a writ is,
and what this practice does that others do not.

### 14. Automate the census

Research path 10 (the root-case census) can be run programmatically. CourtListener
exposes citation relationships through its API `[verify current API coverage]`,
which means in-degree and citing-depth for Texas cases can be computed rather than
read. A script that ranks CCA cases by citations-received against length-of-
original-discussion is a laundering detector, and it costs a weekend.

## Addendum: "the rules of the common law" — as of when?

Three candidate answers. The sources point at the second.

**What is known.** The gap-filler is not a 1965 innovation. It appears in the 1879
Code as art. 27 ("When rules of common law shall govern"), the 1895 Code as art.
26, the 1925 Code as art. 24 ("Common law governs"), and was renumbered 1.27 in
1965; the search summaries say it has been a consistent feature since the original
1856 Code `[verify the 1856 text — the State Law Library has the historical codes
online]`. On the civil side, the reception statute has a settled construction:
*Grigsby v. Reib*, 105 Tex. 597, 153 S.W. 1124 (1913) `[verify]` held the 1840
Act does **not** adopt the common law as applied in England in 1840, but the
English common law **as declared by the courts of the American states** — later
refined as the body of common law *in 1840* as those courts declare it, applied so
far as not inconsistent with Texas conditions. No CCA opinion construing art. 1.27
surfaced by web search; the old article numbers (1879 art. 27, 1925 art. 24) are
the keys for finding one in the annotated codes.

**Reading A — the reception, 1840.** If "the common law" in art. 1.27 means what
it means in the reception statute, the body is fixed at 1840, American, and
adapted. Mostly fine for you, but *Hodge's Case* is English and 1838; the State
would argue the reasonable-hypothesis rule had not yet become *American* common
law by 1840. You would want pre-1840 American authority for the rule, not just
Greenleaf (1846) and Starkie's 1869 American edition.

**Reading B — the enactment, 1856.** The codifiers wrote "the rules of the common
law" in 1856, into a state that had received the common law sixteen years earlier.
Under *Boykin* the words carry their meaning at enactment, and five recodifications
that recite no substantive change carry that meaning forward `[verify the 1965
recodification act's no-substantive-change recital]`. This is the textualist
answer and the best one for you: 1856 captures Hodge, Wills, Greenleaf and Starkie
comfortably, it predates 1876, and it needs no constitutional-freeze theory. It is
also the reading that gives the definite article its force — "**the** rules," a
determinate body, not "such rules as courts may develop."

**Reading C — dynamic.** The State's reading: "the common law" means the common
law as it evolves, including the CCA's own decisions, so *Hankins* and the *Terry*
adoption are simply the common law and art. 1.27 ratifies them. Three answers:
(1) surplusage — under C the provision does no work, since courts fill procedural
gaps by decision regardless, and a reading that leaves a statute idle is
disfavored; (2) *Grigsby* rejected a present-day reading for the reception statute
and anchored the body of law to a date; (3) Penal Code §1.03(a) abolishes
common-law *offenses* — the Legislature excludes the common law when it means to,
so its inclusion for *procedure* is deliberate, and a deliberate inclusion is not
a blank cheque to the court that would be filling the gap anyway.

**The State's better move, and the answer.** Rather than fight the date, the State
will say art. 1.27 does not reach *Terry* at all: the Code does not "fail to
provide" a rule — arts. 14.01–14.04 and 15.22 are the rules — and investigative
detention is a constitutional-floor question, not Code procedure. The answer is
the chain that makes the argument purely Texan: the federal floor *permits*, it
does not *authorize*; authority for a seizure in Texas must come from Texas law;
the Code authorizes arrest (15.22, binary) and nothing between arrest and
liberty; art. 1.27 sends that silence to a common law with no such category; and
**art. 38.23** excludes evidence obtained in violation of the laws of Texas
`[verify text]`, so the detention falls under state exclusion regardless of what
the Fourth Amendment tolerates. Four provisions, no constitutional theory
required — and *Heitman* only as the reason the court is free to read them.
