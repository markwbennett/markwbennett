## 4. Tests that emerged, and that killed most candidates

1. **The phone test.** Someone hears the name spoken and must type it. Kills
   Hephaestus; taxes quaere, inventio, subsilentio. **Note:** this test loses most
   of its force for a sub-brand or a publication, since nobody types those from
   hearing — it governs only the thing on the door.
2. **Whose act does the word name?** *Sub silentio* and *per incuriam* name the
   **court's** failure; *unbriefed* / *unargued* name the **bar's**. Names built
   on someone's omission put blame in the brand. Advocate-side words — inventio,
   assay, question presented, quaere (the critical reader's note) — do not.
3. **Rank claims are the one real rules exposure.** Texas Rule 7.02(a)(4) bars
   comparing your services with other lawyers' unless substantiated by
   verifiable, objective data. *Visionary* asserts a quality; *Leading* asserts a
   rank, which is the exact trigger. Rule 7.01 (rewritten in the amendments
   approved July 1, 2021, now "Communications Concerning a Lawyer's Services")
   permits a trade name that is not false or misleading. **Never claim what you
   can prove**: board certification and the CCA record are substantiable;
   adjectives are not.
4. **The place-noun is a crutch for a vague root.** *Freedom* needs "labs";
   *question presented* does not.
5. **Manufacture vs discovery.** Foundry / forge / smithy / mill promise
   *well-made*. Lab / studio / observatory / station promise *new*. The positioning
   wants the second family.
6. **Name the finding, not the flair.** Imagination / creativity / creation name
   the lawyer's mental flair, which is what an opponent attacks.

## Threats to the argument

### 1. The freeze date is wrong, or at least contestable — and the State will find it

Section 5 says the framers "chose different words in 1876" and thereby adopted the
common law of criminal procedure "as it existed in 1876." But "due course of the
law of the land" was not chosen in 1876. It appears in the **1836 Republic
Constitution** (Declaration of Rights, §7) and the **1845 Constitution** (art. I,
§16) in nearly identical words, and it descends from Magna Carta clause 39 by way
of a dozen other state constitutions. `[verify against the Tarlton texts]`

Consequences:
- The "deliberate choice" premise needs restating. The 1876 framers *readopted*
  inherited language; the argument has to be that readoption carries the 1876
  understanding, or that the relevant date is whenever Texas first adopted the
  phrase — and those lead different places.
- **Footnote 6 is exposed.** *Hodge's Case* is 1838. If the operative adoption is
  1836, the reasonable-hypothesis rule crystallized *after* Texas adopted "due
  course," and the "before 1876, after 1791" window closes for that rule. If the
  date is 1845 or 1876, it holds. The paper should pick a date and defend it before
  the State picks the other one.
- Consider a second anchor: the Republic's statutory adoption of the common law of
  England as the rule of decision (the 1840 act) `[verify]`. It may be the better
  vehicle for "Texas adopted the common law" than the constitutional phrase alone.

This is the root-case problem applied to the manifesto's own root. Do the
archaeology on yourself before the State does.

**Refinement, after pushback:** readoption in 1876 is the right frame, and it
rescues *Hodge's Case* — "the window closes" overstated it; the window closes
only if the court fixes the date at 1836, and readoption supplies a principled
basis for 1876. But Texas's readoption canon is specifically about **judicial
construction**: where a provision that has received a settled judicial
construction is adopted in the same words by a later constitution, the
construction is presumed adopted too (applied to art. I, §10 confrontation, same
words since 1836 — *Robertson v. State* `[verify]`). That is a tool for both
sides. The decisive research question is therefore narrow: **what construction had
"due course of the law of the land" received in Texas cases between 1836 and
1876?** If those courts applied common-law procedure under it, the canon carries
that forward. If they read it as a generic law-of-the-land guarantee, the State
gets the canon.

**And a statutory hook that may be better than the constitutional one:** Tex.
Code Crim. Proc. art. 1.27, "Common Law Governs" — "If this Code fails to provide
a rule of procedure in any particular state of case which may arise, the rules of
the common law shall be applied and govern." Current law. It requires no proof of
a constitutional freeze; the Legislature itself directs that common-law procedure
governs the gaps. Limits: gap-filling only (it yields to codified procedure), and
amendable. But for questions the Code does not address it is a direct textual
argument. The *Terry* argument, for instance, becomes purely statutory: art. 15.22
defines arrest as binary, the Code is silent on any sub-arrest detention, art. 1.27
sends that silence to the common law, and the common law had no such category.
The one open question is *which* common law art. 1.27 incorporates — as of the
Code's adoption, or as it evolves — and that question is worth a post of its own.

### 2. *Hankins* and *Geesa* are two abolitions, and Section 5 runs them together

*Hankins* (1983) abolished the **jury instruction**. *Geesa v. State*, 820 S.W.2d
154 (Tex. Crim. App. 1991) abolished the **reasonable-hypothesis analytical
construct for sufficiency review** `[verify]`. The paper's stated practical
consequence — "every circumstantial-evidence conviction is reviewable under it" —
is a *Geesa* consequence, not a *Hankins* one. And the Bishop passages quoted are
about sufficiency ("the test of the sufficiency of this evidence"), which supports
reviving the *Geesa* construct more directly than the *Hankins* charge.

Two roots, two arguments, two vehicles. Either revive both separately or say which
one, because the State's first move will be to point out that the authorities cited
support the one the paper is not asking for.

### 3. The incorporation premise is asserted, not proven

The treatises prove what the common law *was*. Nothing yet proves that "due course
of law" froze it as a constitutional floor rather than adopting it as a default
rule courts could revise. That is the load-bearing proposition of Sections 5, 6 and
the 1791–1876 window. It needs: the 1875 convention journal, contemporaneous Texas
cases construing "due course," and the other "law of the land" states' treatment of
the same question. Without it, the State's answer is one sentence: the phrase means
what "due process" means, as Texas courts said for a century.

### 4. Floodgates is the court's real objection, and the paper does not answer it

"Every circumstantial-evidence conviction is reviewable" is written as a selling
point. To the Court of Criminal Appeals it is the reason to refuse. A holding that
*Hankins* was void from 1983 unravels four decades of convictions; judges weigh
that whether or not they say so. The argument needs a **remedial theory**: Texas
retroactivity doctrine for new rules on habeas, a prospective-only fallback the
court can grant without opening 1983, and a reason the court should prefer a
narrow first step. Offer the off-ramp or the court will build its own, and it will
be a refusal.

## Threats to the strategy

### 5. Statutory wins are wasting assets

The penalty-group "knowingly" argument, the trespass consent argument, the
§19.02(b) and §43.04(a) unanimity arguments are wins on **statutory text**. The
Legislature meets in January 2027 and can amend any of them into strict liability
or a single offense in one session. Wins on the Texas Constitution cannot be
amended away. The paper already prefers constitutional grounds; the *positioning*
should say so explicitly, and the litigation calendar should assume the State will
ask the Legislature to fix anything that loses.

### 6. There is no forcing mechanism against serial refusal — so manufacture the split

The CCA refused *Solis*, *Earl* and *Riley*. Refusals cost the court nothing and
it can keep refusing. Rule 66.3(a) makes a **conflict among courts of appeals** a
ground for review. *Solis* is in the Fourteenth, *Williams* in the Fourth,
*Johnson* in Dallas — if that distribution is not deliberate, make it deliberate:
put the same issue before different courts, with different panels, until one
splits. A split is the one thing the court cannot refuse indefinitely.

### 7. The federal backstop is asymmetric

The First Amendment work has a real path to the Supreme Court (*Earl* proves it).
The unanimity work does not: *Ramos* requires unanimity on *elements*, but what
counts as an element is state law under *Schad*, and AEDPA deference will defeat a
§2254 claim that a Texas offense definition creates two elements. The unanimity
arguments are **Texas** arguments and should be built to win in Texas, with the
federal overlay as reinforcement rather than as the backstop.

### 8. The Supreme Court of Texas is a flank

The CCA is not the only Texas court reading article I, §19. *Patel v. Texas
Department of Licensing*, 469 S.W.3d 69 (Tex. 2015) held that the due-course clause
has independent content and applied a standard stricter than federal rational
basis `[verify]` — though it assumed rather than decided that the clause applied,
and did not reach the originalist question. The premise "due course of law is not
lockstep with due process" can be strengthened in *civil* cases, where the State
cares less and the Supreme Court of Texas has been aggressively originalist. A
civil holding that the 1876 clause carries the 1876 understanding is persuasive
authority the CCA cannot ignore.

### 9. Publishing moves the *Strickland* baseline

Once the manifesto is public, "no reasonable lawyer would have thought to raise
it" stops being available as a reason failure-to-preserve was not deficient. That
cuts both ways:
- It creates **11.07 vehicles** — ineffective-assistance claims for post-publication
  trials where counsel did not preserve the Texas Constitution objection.
- It creates **exposure for the bar you are recruiting**, and for you, since
  courts generally hold counsel is not deficient for failing to anticipate a change
  in the law — but the paper's whole thesis is that this is not a change in the
  law, it is law that was always there.

Think about whether to say this out loud, because it is the strongest possible
reason for a trial lawyer to make the objection.

### 10. Amici

Novel arguments land with institutional backing. The *Earl* cert petition needs
amici (TCDLA, the First Amendment shops, law professors); so will the first
due-course-of-law PDR the court grants. Lining them up is a year's lead time.

## Addendum: "the rules of the common law" — as of when?

Three candidate answers. The sources point at the second.

**What is known.** The gap-filler is not a 1965 innovation. It appears in the 1879
Code as art. 27 ("When rules of common law shall govern"), the 1895 Code as art.
26, the 1925 Code as art. 24 ("Common law governs"), and was renumbered 1.27 in
1965; the search summaries say it has been a consistent feature since the original
1856 Code `[verify the 1856 text — the State Law Library has the historical codes
online]`. On the civil side, the reception statute has a settled construction:
*Grigsby v. Reib*, 105 Tex. 597, 153 S.W. 1124 (1913) `[verify]` held the 1840
Act does **not** adopt the common law as applied in England in 1840, but the
English common law **as declared by the courts of the American states** — later
refined as the body of common law *in 1840* as those courts declare it, applied so
far as not inconsistent with Texas conditions. No CCA opinion construing art. 1.27
surfaced by web search; the old article numbers (1879 art. 27, 1925 art. 24) are
the keys for finding one in the annotated codes.

**Reading A — the reception, 1840.** If "the common law" in art. 1.27 means what
it means in the reception statute, the body is fixed at 1840, American, and
adapted. Mostly fine for you, but *Hodge's Case* is English and 1838; the State
would argue the reasonable-hypothesis rule had not yet become *American* common
law by 1840. You would want pre-1840 American authority for the rule, not just
Greenleaf (1846) and Starkie's 1869 American edition.

**Reading B — the enactment, 1856.** The codifiers wrote "the rules of the common
law" in 1856, into a state that had received the common law sixteen years earlier.
Under *Boykin* the words carry their meaning at enactment, and five recodifications
that recite no substantive change carry that meaning forward `[verify the 1965
recodification act's no-substantive-change recital]`. This is the textualist
answer and the best one for you: 1856 captures Hodge, Wills, Greenleaf and Starkie
comfortably, it predates 1876, and it needs no constitutional-freeze theory. It is
also the reading that gives the definite article its force — "**the** rules," a
determinate body, not "such rules as courts may develop."

**Reading C — dynamic.** The State's reading: "the common law" means the common
law as it evolves, including the CCA's own decisions, so *Hankins* and the *Terry*
adoption are simply the common law and art. 1.27 ratifies them. Three answers:
(1) surplusage — under C the provision does no work, since courts fill procedural
gaps by decision regardless, and a reading that leaves a statute idle is
disfavored; (2) *Grigsby* rejected a present-day reading for the reception statute
and anchored the body of law to a date; (3) Penal Code §1.03(a) abolishes
common-law *offenses* — the Legislature excludes the common law when it means to,
so its inclusion for *procedure* is deliberate, and a deliberate inclusion is not
a blank cheque to the court that would be filling the gap anyway.

**The State's better move, and the answer.** Rather than fight the date, the State
will say art. 1.27 does not reach *Terry* at all: the Code does not "fail to
provide" a rule — arts. 14.01–14.04 and 15.22 are the rules — and investigative
detention is a constitutional-floor question, not Code procedure. The answer is
the chain that makes the argument purely Texan: the federal floor *permits*, it
does not *authorize*; authority for a seizure in Texas must come from Texas law;
the Code authorizes arrest (15.22, binary) and nothing between arrest and
liberty; art. 1.27 sends that silence to a common law with no such category; and
**art. 38.23** excludes evidence obtained in violation of the laws of Texas
`[verify text]`, so the detention falls under state exclusion regardless of what
the Fourth Amendment tolerates. Four provisions, no constitutional theory
required — and *Heitman* only as the reason the court is free to read them.

## A. Textual differences the lockstep era erased

Article I text differs from the federal text in places, and the dark-ages courts
declared the provisions equivalent without reading the words. These four are the
ones where the difference does work.

### 1. "Give evidence against himself" — art. I, §10

**Rule as applied.** Coextensive with the Fifth Amendment's "be a witness against
himself": testimonial evidence only, so blood draws, breath tests, handwriting
exemplars and lineups are unprotected `[verify: the CCA holding, likely early
1970s, that §10 tracks *Schmerber*]`.

**The gap.** The Texas text says **give evidence**, not *be a witness*. "Evidence"
in 1876 was not a synonym for testimony; Greenleaf's treatise is titled *On
Evidence* and covers real and documentary proof. A citizen compelled to bleed
into a vial gives evidence against himself in the ordinary 1876 sense.

**Why it matters.** Every DWI blood draw, breath test and refusal in the state.
This is the highest-volume textual argument available, and it has the same shape
as due course versus due process — different words, chosen, never read.

### 2. "Possessions" — art. I, §9

**Rule as applied.** Texas follows *Oliver*'s open-fields doctrine and the federal
standing rules `[verify]`.

**The gap.** The Fourth Amendment protects "effects"; §9 protects **possessions**.
Land is a possession; "effects" has never been read to include it. The open-fields
doctrine rests on the federal word. A defendant who *possesses* property he does
not own — a borrowed car, a rented room, a friend's phone — also has a textual
claim to standing the federal "reasonable expectation" test denies.

### 3. "Cruel *or* unusual" — art. I, §13

**Rule as applied.** Coextensive with the Eighth Amendment `[verify: *Cantu v.
State*, late 1990s]`.

**The gap.** The Eighth Amendment is conjunctive; §13 is disjunctive. A punishment
that is merely **unusual** — contrary to long usage, which is what the word meant
in 1876 — violates §13 without also being cruel. Candidates: stacked sentences of
a length no 1876 court could impose, sex-offender conditions with no historical
analogue, punishments for offenses that did not exist. The originalist meaning of
"unusual" is a real literature on the federal side and has never been applied to
the Texas disjunctive.

### 4. "Confronted *with* the witnesses" — art. I, §10

**Rule as applied.** Coextensive with the Confrontation Clause; *Maryland v. Craig*
governs child-witness closed-circuit testimony, and post-2020 practice has
normalized remote testimony `[verify Texas cases]`.

**The gap.** Same words since 1836 — which is exactly the situation the Texas
readoption canon addresses (*Robertson*). What did "confronted with" require in
Texas practice between 1836 and 1876? If it meant physical presence in the
courtroom, then two-way video is a departure the readopted text does not permit,
and *Craig* is a federal floor, not a Texas ceiling.

## B. Lockstep imports never re-examined after *Heitman*

### 5. *Atwater* under art. I, §9

**Rule as applied.** Custodial arrest for a fine-only offense is constitutional
(*Atwater v. Lago Vista*, 2001).

**The gap.** The leading Supreme Court case arose in Texas — and because Atwater
sued in federal court, **the Texas constitutional question was never decided**.
Was custodial arrest for a fine-only, non-breach-of-peace offense permitted at
common law in 1876? Blackstone and the arrest treatises say warrantless arrest lay
for felony and for breach of the peace in the officer's presence; a seatbelt
violation is neither. Art. 14.06's citation-release provision is the statutory
companion. `[verify whether the CCA has addressed §9 post-*Atwater*, and whether
the 1876 argument was made]`

### 6. Community caretaking

**Rule as applied.** Texas adopted *Cady v. Dombrowski*'s community-caretaking
exception (*Wright v. State*, 1999 `[verify]`), permitting a seizure with no
suspicion of any offense.

**The gap.** The common law recognized no authority to seize a person absent an
offense; the doctrine is a 1973 federal innovation adopted by a Texas court that
performed no Texas analysis. It is the cleanest possible target: no offense, no
warrant, no Texas pedigree.

### 7. Pretext

**Rule as applied.** *Whren*; Texas declined a pretext inquiry under §9
(*Crittenden v. State*, 1995 `[verify]`).

**The gap.** The 1876 common law on using an arrest for one purpose to search for
another. If the common law condemned the practice, *Crittenden* is a dark-ages
holding in post-*Heitman* clothing — decided four years after *Heitman* without
doing what *Heitman* authorized.

## C. Manner-or-means, *Apprendi*, *Ramos*

### 8. Continuous sexual abuse — Penal Code §21.02(d)

**Rule as applied.** The statute itself says jurors "are not required to agree
unanimously on which specific acts of sexual abuse were committed." Courts of
appeals have rejected *Richardson v. United States* challenges on the ground that
the acts are "morally and conceptually equivalent" — the *Schad* plurality's
reasoning `[verify; a cert petition, No. 20-973, was filed in January 2021]`.

**The gap.** *Richardson* held that for a federal "series of violations" the jury
must agree on which violations. The Texas courts' answer is *Schad*'s
moral-equivalence test — which is precisely the reasoning *Apprendi* and *Ramos*
displaced, per Section 7 of the manifesto. And if the rejections are courts of
appeals citing each other, the question has been laundered, not settled. Run the
four-part test: has the **CCA** rejected the *Apprendi/Ramos* version after full
briefing? The stakes are 25-to-life with no parole.

### 9. The judge-made family-violence finding

**Rule as applied.** A trial judge makes an affirmative finding of family violence
under art. 42.013; that finding later elevates a misdemeanor assault to a felony
under §22.01(b)(2)(A) `[verify the CCA's *Apprendi* treatment, possibly *Butler v.
State*]`.

**The gap.** *Apprendi*'s prior-conviction exception covers the fact of a prior
conviction. The family-violence *finding* is a separate fact, found by a judge,
not an element of the prior offense, and it increases the punishment range in the
later case. That is the *Apprendi* pattern with the exception's protection
removed. Enormous volume.

### 10. DWI's two definitions of intoxication

**Rule as applied.** "Loss of normal use" (§49.01(2)(A)) and 0.08 (§49.01(2)(B))
are alternative manner-or-means; no unanimity required `[verify: *Bagheri*]`.

**The gap.** Impairment and per-se concentration are different facts proved by
different evidence — one by observation, one by a machine. The manner-or-means
label was applied without the element analysis the manifesto describes. Same
argument as §19.02(b), in the highest-volume offense in the state.

### 11. Aggravated assault's two aggravators — and the "gravamen" root

**Rule as applied.** Serious bodily injury (§22.02(a)(1)) and use of a deadly
weapon (§22.02(a)(2)) are treated under the CCA's "gravamen" jurisprudence
(*Jefferson*, *Landrian*, *Pizzo* `[verify]`).

**The gap.** The gravamen test is judge-made and is the **root proposition** for a
whole family of manner-or-means holdings. It is the natural target for the
root-case census: find where the gravamen test was first announced, read what
that case actually decided, and ask whether the test survives *Apprendi*'s
definition of an element.

## D. Mens rea reach — *Flores-Figueroa* and *Rehaif*

### 12. Felon in possession — Penal Code §46.04

**Rule as applied.** No mental state as to felon status; §6.02 supplies a mental
state for the conduct `[verify CCA treatment]`.

**The gap.** *Rehaif v. United States* (2019) held the federal statute requires
knowledge of the prohibited status. Texas §46.04 states no mental state at all, so
§6.02(b)–(c) supplies one — and the question is whether the supplied mental state
reaches "has been convicted of a felony." The recurring fact pattern is real:
defendants on deferred adjudication, or with out-of-state dispositions, who do
not know their status. Pair with the penalty-group grammar argument; it is the
same move applied to a status element.

### 13. THC concentrate versus marihuana

**Rule as applied.** The Johnson argument, applied to the vape-pen cases.

**The gap.** Marihuana is a Class B misdemeanor; THC concentrate is Penalty Group
2 and a felony. The defendant who believed the cartridge was marijuana did not
"knowingly possess a controlled substance listed in Penalty Group 2." This is the
manifesto's own argument at its highest volume and with its most sympathetic
facts — a teenager with a vape pen — and it should be in the pipeline alongside
*Johnson* rather than after it.

### 14. Failure to register — art. 62.102

**Rule as applied.** "Knowingly" fails to comply; knowledge of the *duty* is not
required `[verify]`.

**The gap.** Same grammar: "knowingly" modifies the failure to comply with a
requirement, and one cannot knowingly fail to comply with a requirement one does
not know exists. The federal SORNA analogue has generated the same argument.

## E. Dormant statutory text

### 15. Art. 1.051's right to counsel

**Rule as applied.** The Texas statutory right to counsel attaches when the Sixth
Amendment right attaches `[verify]`.

**The gap.** Art. 1.051 grants counsel "in an adversarial judicial proceeding" and
art. 15.17 makes magistration a proceeding with the accused before a judicial
officer. If the statute's words reach magistration, the Texas right attaches
earlier than *Rothgery*'s federal floor, and uncounseled statements and waivers
between arrest and appointment are a statutory violation with a 38.23 remedy. A
dormant-text argument of the art. 15.22 kind.

# D. Research paths


Extensions of the three engines in *Making Law*: opinion laundering (find the root),
due course of law (the 1876 common-law incorporation), and manner-or-means versus
elements. Each path gives the hypothesis, the search that tests it, and what would
kill it.

## A. Scaling the dark-ages audit

### 1. Inventory every lockstep import, not just Terry

**Hypothesis.** *Terry*/*Milton* is one instance of a class. Between *Crowell*
(1944) and *Heitman* (1991) the CCA imported federal doctrine wholesale across
search and seizure, self-incrimination, confrontation, double jeopardy, jury trial
and ex post facto. Each import rests on no Texas constitutional analysis, and
*Heitman* reopened all of them — but only the search-and-seizure door has been
tried.

**Search.** Pre-1991 CCA opinions containing "in all material aspects the same" and
its variants; then every case citing *Crowell* or its progeny for the proposition
that a Texas provision means what its federal analog means. Build the list; sort by
how much modern practice rests on each.

**Kills it.** An import that the CCA has re-examined post-*Heitman* on independent
Texas grounds is spent. Check each for a post-1991 independent analysis before
investing.

### 2. The textual-difference inventory

**Hypothesis.** Lockstep interpretation erased textual differences between article I
and the Bill of Rights. Where Texas chose different words, those words were chosen —
"due course" versus "due process" is the proven case, and it will not be the only one.

**Search.** Set article I beside the federal provisions clause by clause. For every
divergence in wording, find the case that declared them equivalent and read whether
it addressed the text at all. The 1875 convention debates and the 1876 ratification
materials are the originalist support.

**Kills it.** A divergence that predates statehood and was copied from another state's
constitution without deliberation is weaker — though still worth the common-law pedigree
argument.

### 3. Borrow from the other "law of the land" states

**Hypothesis.** Texas is not the only state whose nineteenth-century constitution
incorporated common-law criminal procedure. Other state courts have done the
originalist work and assembled the treatise citations already.

**Search.** State constitutions using "law of the land" or "due course of law";
then those states' cases holding the clause incorporates common-law procedure. Their
briefs and opinions are a shortcut to sources, and out-of-state authority costs
nothing to cite as persuasive.

**Kills it.** States whose courts read the clause as identical to federal due process
give you nothing but a contrary citation — know which those are before relying on the family.

## B. Scaling the 1791–1876 window

### 4. Enumerate the window's protections from the treatises

**Hypothesis.** Footnote 6 generalizes. The reasonable-hypothesis instruction is one
protection that crystallized after 1791 and before 1876; the treatises describe dozens.
Each one modern Texas law no longer provides is a due-course-of-law argument.

**Search.** Work Greenleaf, Starkie, Bishop and Wharton systematically rather than for
one rule — table of contents first, then every procedural protection described as
settled. For each, ask: does Texas still provide it? The ones that vanished are the
inventory.

**Kills it.** A protection abolished by *statute* rather than by courts raises the
harder question of legislative power below the constitutional floor. Sort those into a
separate, slower pile.

### 5. The vanished jury instructions

**Hypothesis.** *Hankins* is one of a family. Nineteenth-century Texas juries were
given cautionary instructions that no longer appear in any charge, each abolished by
judicial decision rather than legislative amendment.

**Search.** Pre-1900 Texas opinions via `DA(bef 1900)` reciting jury charges; catalogue
the instructions given as a matter of course. Then trace each forward to the decision
that dropped it. Cross-check which survive by statute (accomplice-witness corroboration
is statutory, so it lives) versus which were purely common-law.

**Kills it.** An instruction that was always discretionary, or that the 1876 courts
themselves split on, will not carry the "settled common law" premise.

### 6. Indictment and pleading practice in 1876

**Hypothesis.** The manner-or-means pleading the State relies on — six methods in one
paragraph — is downstream of a modern relaxation of common-law indictment
requirements. At common law an indictment had to allege the offense with particularity.

**Search.** The treatises on indictment sufficiency; early Texas cases quashing
indictments for failure to specify. Then the twentieth-century Texas cases that relaxed
it, and whether any addressed article I, section 10's notice requirement independently.

**Kills it.** Statutory codification of pleading rules; if the Legislature authorized
the practice, the argument becomes a constitutional-floor argument rather than a
statutory one.

## C. Scaling manner-or-means and mens rea

### 7. The Penal Code verb-list sweep

**Hypothesis.** §19.02(b) and §43.04(a) are examples of a finite, enumerable class:
every offense defined with a list of alternative verbs or circumstances is a candidate
for distinct-element treatment and unanimity.

**Search.** Mechanical, and the highest-yield project here. Walk the Penal Code and the
Health and Safety Code offense definitions; build a table of every offense with
alternatives in the definition; mark which have been litigated as unanimity questions
and which have not. Most will not have been. Rank by frequency of prosecution.

**Kills it.** Where the Legislature grouped alternatives that are genuinely morally
equivalent, *Schad* is a real obstacle — pick the ones where the verbs describe
different conduct by different actors.

### 8. The "knowingly" scope sweep

**Hypothesis.** The penalty-group argument is *Flores-Figueroa* applied to one statute.
Any offense where a mental state precedes a list including a circumstance element has
the same structure, and the pattern charge probably drops the circumstance.

**Search.** Offense definitions where a culpable mental state is followed by an object
containing a qualifying phrase (a classification, a status, an age, a value, a location).
For each, read the pattern charge's application paragraph and see whether the qualifier
survives.

**Kills it.** Where the Legislature separately defined the circumstance as a strict-
liability aggravator, the grammar argument weakens — but that itself is an Apprendi
question.

### 9. Audit the pattern charges against the statutes

**Hypothesis.** The State Bar pattern charges are a finite corpus, and every divergence
between an application paragraph and its statute is a potential egregious-harm argument.
Two divergences are already documented (penalty group, trespass consent). There is no
reason to think there are only two.

**Search.** Line up each pattern charge beside its offense definition and diff them.
This is grunt work with a very high hit rate, and it produces trial-level arguments that
require no appellate posture to deploy.

**Kills it.** Divergences that *narrow* the offense favor the defense and should be left
alone.

## D. Scaling the laundering detector

### 10. The root-case census

**Hypothesis.** Laundered lines have a signature: a root case with enormous in-degree
whose own discussion of the proposition is a sentence or two, and descendants that cite
the root rather than reasoning independently.

**Search.** Pick a doctrine, pull the citator's full citing list, and sort by depth of
treatment. The nodes with thousands of citations and shallow treatment in the root are
the candidates. Then read the root. *Aguirre* is the proof of concept; the method is
general and mostly mechanical.

**Kills it.** A root whose reasoning is actually adequate — which happens, and finding
out early is cheap.

### 11. The dormant-statute sweep

**Hypothesis.** Article 15.22 is not the only statute a Texas court has declared
obsolete without a repeal. Each such statute is dormant: still binding, waiting to be
applied.

**Search.** Opinions containing "legislatively obsolete," "no longer viable," "has been
superseded," "of no further effect," and similar, paired with a statutory citation.
Then read the statute and ask what it would do if applied as written.

**Kills it.** An actual repeal or amendment. Check the statutory history first — it takes
a minute and saves the argument from embarrassment.

### 12. Abolitions that parallel *Hankins*

**Hypothesis.** The *Hankins* argument — that the court lacked power to abolish a
constitutionally incorporated protection — applies to every Texas-specific protection the
CCA abolished by decision rather than by legislative amendment.

**Search.** Cases in which the CCA eliminated a defense-favorable Texas rule and joined a
federal or majority trend. The abolition of factual-sufficiency review in *Brooks v.
State* `[verify: 323 S.W.3d 893 (Tex. Crim. App. 2010) (plurality)]` is the obvious
candidate and structurally identical: a Texas-specific protection, abolished by the
court, justified by reference to the federal standard rather than to Texas constitutional
analysis. Ask the same question — was the protection part of the common law of 1876, or
otherwise beyond the court's power to remove?

**Kills it.** A protection that originated in a statute later repealed, or one the court
can fairly characterize as its own procedural innovation, never had constitutional
footing to begin with.

# E. Per incuriam notes


## What this session could actually do

Outbound network here is restricted to GitHub. No Westlaw, no Lexis, no fetching
opinion text — not even from txcourts.gov or CourtListener, which are blocked at
the proxy. Everything below comes from **web-search result summaries**, which
means: treat every citation as a lead to verify, not as verified law. I have not
read any of these opinions.

## Finding 1: no Texas use of "per incuriam" surfaced

Several searches, including ones restricted to txcourts.gov, courtlistener.com
and law.justia.com, produced **no Texas Court of Criminal Appeals or Texas court
of appeals opinion using the phrase**. The hits were all dictionaries, the
Wikipedia entry, English and Indian cases, and — repeatedly — American discussion
of *per curiam*, which search engines conflate with it.

That is a weak negative. Web search is not full-text case search, and a phrase
buried in a 1987 opinion would never surface this way. **The real test is a
full-text query you can run in a minute:**

```
Westlaw:  "per incuriam"           (database: TX-CS, then TX-CS-CRIM)
Lexis:    "per incuriam"           (Texas Cases, All)
Free:     scholar.google.com > Case law > Texas > "per incuriam"
```

If that comes back empty or near-empty in Texas, that is itself the finding — and
it is the interesting one.

## Finding 2: Texas already has the doorway, under a different name

The more useful result. The CCA's own stare decisis framework reportedly includes
a factor for precedent that was **"flawed from the outset."** Search summaries
attribute the three-factor articulation to:

> *Hammock v. State*, 46 S.W.3d 889, 892-93 (Tex. Crim. App. 2001)

with the factors given as: (1) the original rule was flawed from the outset;
(2) the older precedent conflicts with a newer, more soundly reasoned decision;
(3) the rule consistently produces unjust results or places unnecessary burdens
on the system. Summaries also attach similar factors to *Ex parte Lewis*.

**Verification status:** I confirmed that *Hammock v. State*, 46 S.W.3d 889, is a
real May 23, 2001 CCA decision (it granted PDR on whether a limiting instruction
must be requested when the evidence comes in, and reaffirmed *Garcia v. State*,
887 S.W.2d 862 (Tex. Crim. App. 1994)). That fits — a court declining to overrule
its own precedent is exactly where it would set out when overruling *is* proper.
But **I have not read 892-93 and cannot confirm the quoted factors or the pin
cite.** Read it before it goes in a brief.

One more unverified thread worth chasing: the summaries also say the burden is
heavier where the court is asked to overrule a point of **statutory
construction** than a judge-made rule. If true, it decides which of these fights
are worth taking.

## Finding 3: the strategy this suggests

You do not need to import *per incuriam* by name, and probably should not lead
with it. Texas supplies the doorway — "flawed from the outset" — and the English
doctrine supplies the **mechanism** that explains *why* a rule can be flawed from
the outset: the original court was never shown the statute or the controlling
decision, so it never confronted the question at all.

That is a two-step argument: a Texas factor the CCA already recognizes, plus a
reason it applies here that nobody has articulated, with *Young v Bristol
Aeroplane Co Ltd* [1944] KB 718 and the Commonwealth line available as persuasive
reasoning rather than authority. It is not a stretch and it is not a gimmick —
it is an explanation for the factor the court already applies.

## The queries that actually find fifty-year gaps

More valuable than hunting the Latin. These find the places where the CCA has
repeatedly declined to decide something:

```
"assuming without deciding"      /p  <your issue>
"we need not decide"             /p  <your issue>
"assuming arguendo"              /p  <your issue>
"has never squarely"  or  "not squarely addressed"
"without the benefit of"  /s  brief!
"sub silentio"           /s  precedent
"flawed from the outset"                 -> the whole CCA stare decisis line
```

The first three are the mechanical version of your thesis: every "assuming
without deciding" in a line of cases is a question the court stepped over, and if
the step-over has been repeated for decades, the underlying question has never
been briefed properly to anyone.

A citator pass does the other half: take the foundational case in a line, pull
everything citing it, and look for the tell — the original decided the point in a
sentence with no reasoning, and everything since cites the original rather than
reasoning independently. That is a per incuriam pattern whether or not Texas has
ever used the phrase.

## The ten

### 1. Nobody knows what they're doing, and that is permission

**Thesis:** The appellate courts, the prosecutors, the defense bar — nobody has
ever known what they were doing, so everything that came before is subject to
challenge.

The paper's actual opening claim and the license for everything after it: "I sure
don't know what I'm doing; I treat that admission as permission." Provocative,
true, and it reframes ignorance as standing to challenge rather than a reason to
defer. Nothing else here works if a reader rejects this.

### 2. The argument that has not been made is not wrong, because it has not been made

**Thesis:** Absence of precedent is not adverse precedent — and "the absence of a
published opinion rejecting the argument is evidence that nobody has made it, but
not that it has been considered and properly rejected."

A claim about how to read silence in the reports, and the single most portable
idea in the paper. Every lawyer has abandoned an argument on the reasoning this
demolishes. Pairs with the diagnosis that good lawyers are *insufficiently
arrogant* — they assume someone else had the idea and had reasons to drop it.

### 3. Opinion laundering: one root case, forty-two descendants

**Thesis:** A proposition analyzed once, if at all, acquires the appearance of
dense support through generations of interlocking citations back to a root case
that may have held something narrower, or answered a different question.

Lead with the specimen in footnote 8 rather than the abstraction: *Hankins* gave
three reasons for abolishing the reasonable-hypothesis instruction and all three
came from *Holland* — "cite Holland, then pretend that its arguments are your
own." Laundering caught inside a single footnote.

### 4. "Settled" has four conditions, and they are usually unmet

**Thesis:** Texas criminal law is settled only when the Court of Criminal Appeals
has rejected *this specific argument*, on facts like these, after full briefing on
the point.

Unpublished opinions do not bind; published opinions bind one district; and a
binding opinion that never addressed your argument is not binding against it.
A checklist readers can run before conceding anything.

### 5. The archaeology: trace the proposition to its root

**Thesis:** Reversing opinion laundering is a procedure, not a talent — find the
root case, read what it actually said, then ask whether it doesn't apply here, was
wrong then, or is wrong now.

Use *Aguirre* as the worked example: every court of appeals treats §19.02(b)(1)
and (b)(2) as alternative means on its authority, when what *Aguirre* decided was
a general-verdict question and the unanimity question "was not before the court."
Separate the three frames readers conflate — conjunctive/disjunctive pleading,
general verdict, unanimity.

### 6. An iterative strategy survives — and exploits — losses

**Thesis:** A loss can be the instrument. *Barton* and *Sanders* forced the Court
of Criminal Appeals to overturn the statute or take an untenable position; it took
the untenable one; *Counterman* removed its premise; *Owens* contradicted it on
the page; *Earl* is the cert vehicle the losses built.

**My worst omission first time through, and probably the best thesis in the
paper.** Nobody writes this down: how to lose a PDR usefully, and why the lawyer
who stopped at *Barton* loses while the lawyer who keeps going might win. The
twelve-year timeline from the start of the 42.07 litigation to *Owens* is the
honest part — it tells readers what this actually costs.

### 7. The 1791–1876 gap

**Thesis:** Federal due process froze in 1791; Texas due course of law was adopted
in 1876 — so every common-law protection that crystallized in between was
incorporated into the Texas Constitution and never into the federal one.

This is footnote 6, generalized, and it deserves the main text. *Hodge's Case* is
1838: after the Fifth Amendment, before article I, section 19. The footnote proves
it for the reasonable-hypothesis instruction; the thesis is that the same
eighty-five-year window contains everything else the nineteenth-century common law
worked out. It converts a one-off argument into a research program.

### 8. *Heitman* handed the bar a revolution in 1991 and the bar declined it

**Thesis:** Thirty-plus years of Texas constitutional independence have gone
largely unused because defense lawyers cited the provision without argument,
analysis, or authority — "Lawyers had walked up to the open door, shrugged their
shoulders in bafflement, and turned around."

An indictment of the bar that doubles as an invitation, and the best possible
setup for the research-methodology post (pre-1900 opinions via `DA(bef 1900)`,
founding-era treatises, English and early American cases).

### 9. A conservative Court of Criminal Appeals is a defense asset

**Thesis:** "Most appellate lawyers see a CCA composed of political conservatives
and conclude they cannot win. That is backward." Scalia's originalism produced
Crawford, Apprendi, Blakely and Booker; originalism applied to the Texas
Constitution of 1876 produces these arguments.

Counterintuitive, checkable, and immediately actionable — it changes who a reader
writes their brief for. Carry Yeary's *Navarro* line: "less stare decisis and more
recte decide."

### 10. A court cannot repeal a statute by calling it obsolete

**Thesis:** The Court of Criminal Appeals called article 15.22's binary definition
of arrest "legislatively obsolete" in *Sheppard*; that is faithless to the
Legislature's language. "The statute is dormant: its words still bind, waiting for
a court to apply them."

Small, sharp, and portable well beyond article 15.22 — a general principle about
judicial power over statutory text, with an immediate application in every
suppression hearing.

1. Cookie-Cutter Practice and the Problem of Opinion Laundering Most criminal appeals are losers. If
they aren’t outright Anders cases, there was nothing to preserve at trial, and the only error
preserved is harmless. Most criminal appellate lawyers practice the same way. We read the record,
identify the objections, find the things that didn’t need to be preserved, look up the case law, and
write the brief. That brief reports what the courts have said; it does not examine whether what the
courts have said is correct. A harmful proposition of law appears in one early case: call that the
root case. The next generation of cases cites the root case. The generation after 4 that cites those
cases. By the fourth generation, 42 cases appear to stand for the proposition, with extensive cross-
support in each, and every new lawyer looking at the issue finds those 42 cases and concludes the
law is settled. I call this opinion laundering. The question is analyzed once, if at all, but with
generations of interlocking citations to other cases that cite each other and the root case, the
courts make it appear that the opinion has more support than that one case. The root case may have
held something narrower. It may have been about a different question entirely. The root case was
probably decided when everything was different. Nobody checks, because everyone else has agreed that
the root is correct, and it’s a little bit arrogant to presume that everyone else is wrong. We find
winning arguments by being a little bit arrogant, and reversing the opinion-laundering process.
Trace the proposition back to its root. Read what the root case actually said. Figure out why it
doesn’t apply to this situation, or why it was wrong then, or why it’s wrong now. Opinion laundering
is the ordinary product of lawyers not doing this archaeology. A case is cited for what it is
assumed to say. The next lawyer finds the citing case and assumes the same. Nobody returns to the
source.

2. What ‘Settled’ Means We know that an unpublished court of appeals opinion is not binding
precedent. A published opinion is not binding in other districts. But consider this: An otherwise-
binding published court of appeals opinion from your 5 district that did not address your specific
argument is not binding precedent against that argument. Even a Court of Criminal Appeals opinion
does not settle an argument the court never considered. The law is “settled,” for our purposes, only
when the Court of Criminal Appeals has rejected the specific argument we intend to make, on facts
like ours, after full briefing on the point. Those conditions are often unmet.1 None of the
arguments in this paper have been rejected yet; most have barely been made.

the Strategy There are three steps. First, take your own idea seriously. When you find a doctrinal
question that has been answered without analysis, the answer is open. Second, find the path. A
single case will not always produce the win. Lo did not produce Earl directly; it produced a model
that, through Barton and Sanders and Counterman, eventually produced Earl. The path is rarely short.
It requires patience and persistence. These are years-long projects. Owens came a dozen years after
we started litigating the First Amendment unconstitutionality of the electronic-harassment statute.
Third, write for textualist and originalist judges. The current Court of Criminal Appeals is
receptive to arguments grounded in the text and original meaning of the Texas Constitution.
Articulate your argument in those terms. Cite the framers’ treatises. Cite the early cases. Show the
genealogy. The originalist judge who reads your brief sees the same text, the same history, the same
logic that you do. Judge Yeary has written to that effect more than once. In Holder v. State, 595
S.W.3d 691, 705 (Tex. Crim. App. 2020) (Yeary, J., concurring): “The Texas Constitution means what
it means, and that meaning does not vary on the whim of a majority of Justices sitting at any given
moment on the United States Supreme Court.” Five years later, in Navarro v. State, 726 S.W.3d 436,
459 (Tex. Crim. App. 2025) (Yeary, J., concurring, joined by Schenck, P.J.): “the judges of this
country and of this state need to enforce less stare 11 decisis and more recte decide,” which he
glossed in a footnote as “make the right decision.”

4. The Jury Charge That Doesn’t Track the Statute 4.1. The Manner-or-Means Insight, Applied to Drug
Cases Under the federal Controlled Substances Act, “It shall be unlawful for any person knowingly or
intentionally to possess [or manufacture, distribute, dispense, or possess with intent to
manufacture, distribute, or dispense] a controlled substance.” 21 U.S.C. § 844. “Knowingly” reaches
“a controlled substance”—the defendant has to know that the substance is some controlled substance.
Under the Texas statute, by contrast, the offense is to (for example) “knowingly or intentionally
possesses a controlled substance listed in Penalty Group 1 or 1-B.” “Knowingly” reaches not only
“controlled substance,” but also (for example) “in Penalty Group 1 or 1-B.” Grammatically, for the
same reason the federal defendant has to know that the substance is a controlled substance, the
state defendant has to know that the substance is one of those in that penalty group. 4.2. The
Missing Element The State Bar’s pattern jury charge does not agree, and every controlled-substance
jury charge for the last fifty years is probably wrong. The application paragraph of the standard
charge reads: the defendant is guilty if he “knowingly possessed a controlled substance, namely,
[drug name].” The 12 statute reads: a person commits an offense if he “knowingly … possesses a
controlled substance listed in Penalty Group 1 or 1-B.”3 “Listed in Penalty Group 1-B” is missing
from the standard charge. The penalty-group classification is a fact that “knowingly” reaches; the
jury must find that the defendant knew the identity of the substance (as one in the penalty group).
A defendant who thought he had a drug in Penalty Group 3 did not “knowingly possess a controlled
substance in Penalty Group 1-B”, and did not commit the crime defined by the Legislature. The
standard charge does not permit the jury to acquit a defendant on such grounds. 4.3. The Case That
Surfaced the Problem The argument surfaced recently in a Dallas Court of Appeals case involving a
defendant who was carrying (allegedly possessing with intent to deliver) fentanyl, but whose defense
was that he believed he was carrying Xanax instead.4 The charge required the jury to find that the
defendant knowingly possessed a controlled substance, and to find that the substance was fentanyl,
but did not require the jury to find that the defendant knowingly possessed a controlled substance
in Penalty Group 1-B. The charge left 3. Tex. Health & Safety Code § 481.115(a), (d) (possession of

5. The Elimination of Reasonable-Hypothesis Review In 1983, the Court of Criminal Appeals abolished
a Texas-specific protection for criminal defendants in circumstantial-evidence cases: the
reasonable-hypothesis-of-innocence standard. Hankins v. State, 646 S.W.2d 191 (Tex. Crim. App.
1983), dispensed with the reasonable-hypothesis-of-innocence jury charge, the cautionary instruction
telling the jury that to convict on circumstantial evidence, it must exclude every reasonable
hypothesis of innocence consistent with the proven facts. After Hankins, juries were no longer
instructed on that rule, displacing a Texas standard dating back to 1876 and beyond. Our argument
here is that the Hankins abolition was beyond the court’s power. 5.1. The Common-Law Pedigree The
reasonable-hypothesis standard for circumstantial evidence has a long common-law pedigree. The
English source is Hodge’s Case, 2 Lewin 227, 168 Eng. Rep. 1136 (Cromp. Assizes 1838), where Baron
Alderson instructed the jury that on circumstantial evidence the inculpatory facts must be
incompatible with the prisoner’s innocence and incapable of explanation upon any other reasonable
hypothesis than that of his guilt.6 The American writers picked it up. William Wills, in his Essay

Constitution’s due-course-of-law provision, in article I, section 19, adopted the common law of
criminal procedure as it existed in 1876. As far as current criminal procedure has diverged from
that common law, it is unconstitutional under the Texas Constitution. The Court of Criminal Appeals
interprets the Constitution, but cannot change it. If the reasonable-hypothesis-of-innocence
standard was part of the common law of criminal procedure that the Texas Constitution adopted in
1876, then it is a constitutional standard that the Court of Criminal Appeals cannot—whether by
rulemaking or by caselaw—abrogate. 19 Hankins was not authorized by legislative amendment to any
statute. If the standard it abolished was constitutional, Hankins was void. 5.5. Hankins’s
Justification (and Why It’s Wrong) Hankins asserted three reasons for abolishing the cautionary

are equally probative; there is no reason to treat one with greater caution than the other. Third,
the cautionary instruction “serves only to distract jurors from examining the proper standard of
proof as the primary focus of their deliberations.”8 All three reasons fail as a matter of
constitutional structure. The federal-trend argument is irrelevant to a Texas constitutional
question. The Texas Constitution of 1876 adopted the common law of criminal procedure as it then
existed. The cautionary instruction was part of that common law, as the subsections above
demonstrate. What other states later did with their evidentiary instructions does not bear on what
the Texas Constitution permits.

Texas Constitutional Law The primary test case for the Texas constitutional argument is the
detention. Texas courts recognize a category of police-citizen encounter called a “detention”—a
restriction of liberty less than an arrest, supported by reasonable suspicion rather than probable
cause. That framework comes directly from the United States Supreme Court’s decision in Terry v.
Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). It has no independent Texas constitutional pedigree. The CCA adopted it
during the dark ages, wholesale, from federal law. The common law of criminal procedure did not
recognize a category of police-citizen encounter between a consensual encounter and an arrest. The
distinction was binary: either you were arrested or you were free to go. An arrest required probable
cause. A sub-arrest detention requiring only reasonable suspicion is a federal common-law innovation
foreign to Texas constitutional law. The common-law definition of arrest, unbroken from at least
1619 to 1853, supports the binary. Blackstone, Commentaries 286 (1769), defined arrest as “the
apprehending or restraining of one’s person, in order to be forthcoming to answer an alleged or
suspected crime.” Archbold, Practice, Pleading, and Procedure in Criminal Cases 21 (1853), repeated
the definition almost verbatim twenty-three years before the Texas Constitution. See also Dalton,
The Country Justice, Arrest, and Imprisonment (1619) (carrying the same definition back two
centuries earlier, to the Statute of Winchester era); Burn, 25 Justice of the Peace, Arrest (1845)
(same definition, contemporaneous with the Republic of Texas’s adoption of the common law). Each
treatise treats restraint of liberty as the trigger. None recognizes a sub-restraint category.
Consider the etymology. “Arrest” means to stop. “Detain” means to hold. You cannot hold what you
have not stopped; logically, a detention requires an arrest. At common law, therefore, there was no
such thing as a detention that was not an arrest. And an arrest required probable cause. The
“reasonable suspicion” standard that Terry v. Ohio created is a departure from the common law. If
the Texas Constitution of 1876 adopted the common law, and the common law required probable cause
for any seizure of the person, then Texas constitutional law does not authorize the reasonable-
suspicion detention that Texas courts have been applying for fifty years. The argument has never
been made. 6.5. A Vehicle: Roberts v. State Roberts v. State—pending in the Fourteenth Court of
Appeals, cause 14-25-00061-CR—is one vehicle. The case has three legal layers. Roberts, a lawyer,
came to the Brazoria County jail to visit someone. Detention staff prevented him from leaving and
conducted a search, more extensive than a patdown but less than a full strip. The trial court denied
suppression. The State defended on Terry-frisk and jail-administration grounds—both rooted in
federal Fourth Amendment doctrine. Neither rests on Texas constitutional analysis. 26 The three
layers are independent, and each is sufficient by itself. First, statutory. Texas Code of Criminal
Procedure article 14.03(a)(1) requires that a warrantless arrest rest on probable cause that the
person has committed an offense or breached the peace.11 There is no “detention” between consensual
encounter and arrest in the Texas statute. A peace officer who restrains a person’s liberty has
either made an arrest (probable cause required) or has not (the person is free to go). The Brazoria
County deputies prevented Roberts from leaving. By the statute, that was an arrest. They had no
probable cause. Second, constitutional. Article I, section 9 of the Texas Constitution required
probable cause for the seizure of the person at common law in 1876, as Section 5 above demonstrates.
The 1876 Constitution adopted that common law. Texas’s subsequent adoption of the Terry framework,
in Milton v. State, 549 S.W.2d 190 (Tex. Crim. App. 1977), was a dark-ages import from federal
Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. Milton did not survive Heitman in any principled sense. Judge Yeary
has signaled the same direction in concurring opinions. Third, structural. Article 15.22 of the Code
of Criminal Procedure provides that a person is arrested when he has been actually placed under
restraint or 11. Tex. Const. art. I, § 9; Tex. Code Crim. Proc. art. 14.03(a)(1) (a peace officer

sub-arrest detention, article 15.22 does what it always did: defines any restraint of liberty as an
arrest, requiring probable cause. Texas adopted Terry by judicial fiat during the dark period; the
adoption rests on no Texas constitutional analysis at all. Roberts therefore might present the
textualist and originalist Court of Criminal Appeals—through the Fourteenth Court of Appeals—with
three independent grounds to hold that Texas constitutional law does not authorize the sub-arrest
detention. There are probably better cases for the argument—it was not perfectly preserved in the
trial court, and we win on other grounds—but the briefing is done. 6.6. The Moment This argument has

does not require juror unanimity on the alternative means by which an offense is committed. The
plurality treated premeditated murder and felony murder as morally-equivalent alternative means and
held the jury did not have to agree 32 on which. The State will cite Schad in any case raising the
unanimity arguments in this paper. Nine years after Schad, Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466
(2000), clarified the rule. After Apprendi, the line between means and element is the line between
facts that may be left out of the State’s burden (facts which, proved, do not increase the
punishment range) and facts that must be submitted to the jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt
(facts which, proved, do). The penalty-group classification in a Texas controlled-substance case is
essential to conviction of a controlled-substance offense in that penalty group. The “agrees”
element in Texas Penal Code section 43.02(c-1)(2)(A) is essential to conviction of solicitation of a
child rather than the misdemeanor variant. Sections 19.02(b)(1) and (b)(2) describe different
essential facts that distinguish one offense from another. Each is an element; Schad’s “means”
analysis does not reach them. 7.2. Why the Best Lawyers Missed It Why have the best criminal

8. Criminal Trespass: Without the Effective Consent of Whom? Criminal trespass under Texas Penal
Code section 30.05 is a small example. It belongs here because the small example proves the same
point as the larger ones: the State systematically narrows statutory text to make conviction easier,
and nobody objects. The statute makes it an offense to enter or remain on or in property of another
“without effective consent.”13 That element is satisfied only when no one with authority to consent
has consented. It is not satisfied when one authorized person has refused consent and another
authorized person has given it. If two roommates jointly possess an apartment and one tells you to
leave but the other says you can stay, the second roommate’s consent makes your remaining lawful.
The statute says so. The standard charge in Harris County and elsewhere does not say so. It alleges
that the defendant entered or remained “without the effective consent of [a specific named person].”
Substituting one person’s name for the statutory “effective consent” narrows the offense. It
transforms a defense based on consent from any authorized person into a defense based on consent
only from the named person. The standard charge writes a different statute than the Legislature
wrote. 13. Tex. Penal Code § 30.05(a) (a person commits an offense if the person enters or remains
on or in property of another “without effective consent”). 34 The remedy is the remedy in every
section of this paper. Object on the record. Cite the statutory text. Cite article I, section 19 of
the Texas Constitution and the due-course-of-law incorporation of the common law. Argue that the
standard charge does not track the statute. The objection costs nothing. The error will rarely be
preserved because criminal-trespass cases are rarely tried. The manner-or-means insight reaches
misdemeanors too. The trespass charge does at the small-stakes end what Williams, Solis, and Johnson
do at the felony end: it substitutes one fact (a named person’s refusal of consent) for the
statutory element (the absence of any authorized consent). No vehicle has reached the Court of
Criminal Appeals, because criminal trespass charges get dismissed instead of tried. That makes the
trial lawyer’s job the whole job here. Preserve the objection on the right facts—two or more
authorized persons could have given consent, the State is relying on only one—and the PDR will
follow when a properly preserved conviction holds up long enough for appellate review.

9. Murder Unanimity: Aguirre Doesn't Hold What Courts Say It Holds The manner-or-means insight
forces a unanimity consequence in murder cases. Texas Penal Code section 19.02(b) defines two
offenses. Section 19.02(b)(1) is intentional murder: the defendant intentionally or knowingly causes
the death of an individual. Under section 19.02(b)(2) the defendant intends to cause serious bodily
injury, commits an act clearly dangerous to human life, and that act causes death. The mental states
are different. The 35 conduct elements are different. They are separate offenses, and the jury must
be unanimous on which one was committed. Every Texas court of appeals has held otherwise. Each court
treats sections 19.02(b)(1) and (b)(2) as alternative ways of committing the same offense—as manner-
or-means—and so does not require unanimity on which subsection the jury found. The error is uniform.
The reasoning in each case traces back to one citation: Aguirre v. State, as if Aguirre had decided
the question. Aguirre did not decide the unanimity question. The case involved a murder prosecution
in which the defendant fired a shotgun through the door of his ex-wife’s house, killing his daughter
Elizabeth, who was inside. The indictment charged Elizabeth’s death in two paragraphs: one alleging
intentional/knowing murder under section 19.02(a)(1); the other alleging felony murder under section
19.02(a)(3) with criminal mischief as the predicate. The court held that the doctrine of merger did
not bar the felony-murder prosecution; that intent to kill the ex-wife transferred to the actual
killing of the daughter for purposes of section 19.02(a)(1); and that the judgment properly
described the jury’s findings. Every court of appeals that has cited Aguirre for the unanimity
proposition has read it wrong. What Aguirre decided, relevant to the jury charge, was the general-
verdict question. The court held that the indictment “did not allege different offenses but only
alleged different ways of committing the same offense,” and so approved a general verdict form.
Aguirre, 732 S.W.2d at 326. Whether 36 the jurors had to unanimously agree on which theory was a
question that was not before the court. The courts of appeals citing Aguirre for the unanimity
proposition pull the “same offense” language out of its general-verdict context and use it to answer
a question Aguirre did not reach. Two doctrinal frames are sometimes confused with the unanimity
question. The conjunctive-or-disjunctive-pleading question asks whether an indictment that joins
multiple statutory methods with “and” allows the State to prove any one of them. The general-verdict
question asks whether the jury must specify which theory it accepted or may return an unspecified
“guilty.” The unanimity question asks whether sections 19.02(b)(1) and 19.02(b)(2) are separate
offenses. If they are, the jury must agree on which one was committed, but they don’t have to tell
us (in a special verdict) which they chose. Solis v. State was a recent attempt to correct the
intermediate courts’ misreading of Aguirre. The trial court instructed the jury that it could
convict if it found either subsection, without requiring unanimity on which. The Fourteenth Court of
Appeals affirmed, citing the same line of cases that traces back to a misread Aguirre. The Court of
Criminal Appeals refused review on January 15, 2026. The next vehicle is still needed.14 Apprendi v.
New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), defined elements (things that must be proven to the factfinder) as
any fact (except, possibly, the fact of a
